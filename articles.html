Managing a Team vs. Leading One

Leadership Fundamentals

Every student organization runs on some mix of management and leadership, but the two aren't the same skill. Management keeps a team on schedule. Leadership gives a team a reason to stay on schedule when it stops being easy.

Management is task-oriented. It's assigning roles, tracking deadlines, following up on deliverables, and making sure logistics don't fall through the cracks. It's necessary — a group with no management structure will miss deadlines no matter how inspired everyone feels. But management alone treats people like items on a checklist, and checklists don't ask why they're doing something.

Leadership is direction-oriented. It's explaining why a task matters, not just that it's due Friday. It's noticing when someone is quietly burning out before they say anything. It's the difference between “submit the design by Friday” and “here's why this design has to land — it's the first thing new members will see, and it sets the tone for the whole event.” Same deadline, very different reason to care.

Most student leaders default to management under pressure because it's measurable — you can point to a spreadsheet and see what's late. Leadership is harder to track, which is exactly why it gets skipped when things get busy. But teams that only experience management tend to do the minimum required and disengage the moment no one's watching.

The fix isn't choosing one over the other — it's noticing which one you default to and deliberately practicing the other. If you're naturally good at rallying people but bad at tracking deadlines, build a simple tracking system. If you're naturally organized but rarely check in on how people are doing, block ten minutes before every meeting just to ask.

How to Make Good Decisions Without All the Information

Leadership Fundamentals

Student leadership rarely comes with complete information. A vendor cancels the day before an event. A key volunteer drops out mid-planning. A budget gets cut with no warning. Waiting for certainty in these moments isn't caution — it's a decision by default, and usually the worst one available.

A useful first question is whether the decision is reversible. If a wrong call can be corrected later with reasonable effort, it's worth deciding quickly and adjusting if needed. If it's irreversible — signing a contract, publicly announcing a date, spending a large portion of the budget — it deserves more caution, even if that means asking for more time.

A second question is who actually needs to be consulted. It's tempting to loop in the whole team for every hard call, but that usually just delays the decision and diffuses the responsibility for it. A smaller, trusted group — the people closest to the specific problem — can usually decide faster and with better context than a full committee.

It also helps to set a “good enough” threshold instead of an ideal one. Perfect information rarely arrives in time to be useful. A decision made with 70 percent of the picture, acted on quickly, usually beats a decision made with 95 percent of the picture two days too late.

Whatever you decide, write down the reasoning in a sentence or two, even informally. It protects you if the decision is questioned later, and it makes the next similar decision easier because you're not starting from zero. Confidence under uncertainty isn't about being right every time — it's about being able to explain why the call made sense with what you knew at the time.

5 Signs Your Event Timeline Is Too Tight

Event Planning Essentials

A packed program looks impressive on paper. The problem is paper doesn't account for late attendees, a microphone that needs troubleshooting, or a segment that runs four minutes over because the crowd was into it. Here are five signs your timeline needs more breathing room before it's tested live.

  1. Every task has exactly one person who can do it. If your emcee is also your tech operator and your only person who knows the Wi-Fi password, one delay anywhere becomes a delay everywhere. Tight timelines have no redundancy built in.
  2. Setup and rehearsal share the same block. If your schedule assumes the venue will be fully set up and the run-through finished in the same 30 minutes, one of those two things is going to get cut short — usually the rehearsal, which is the one that actually catches mistakes.
  3. There's no transition time between segments. Real programs don't move from one segment straight into the next with zero gap. People need to walk on and off stage, hand off microphones, and reset. A timeline with zero transition buffer is a timeline that's already behind before the event starts.
  4. Vendor and supplier arrival times are assumed, not confirmed. "They should be here by 8" is not the same as a confirmed delivery window. If your timeline depends on an external party showing up exactly on schedule, and that hasn't been confirmed in writing, the plan is built around a hope.
  5. There's no answer for what happens if something runs ten minutes over. If the honest answer to "what do we cut if we're running late" is "we'll figure it out," that's the clearest sign the timeline is too tight — because the plan already assumes it might not hold.

None of this means over-engineering every minute. It means building in small amounts of slack on purpose, in the places most likely to slip, so a single delay doesn't cascade into the whole program falling apart.

The Logistics Checklist Every Student Event Needs

Event Planning Essentials

Most event failures aren't caused by one big mistake — they're caused by a handful of small things nobody thought to check. A simple checklist, reviewed a few days out and again the morning of, catches most of them before they become a problem on the day.

Venue & Permits

  • Confirm the booking in writing, not just verbally
  • Check what's actually included — tables, chairs, sound system, power outlets
  • Confirm access time for setup, not just the event start time
  • Verify any required permits or clearances are signed off

Program Flow

  • Walk through the schedule minute by minute, not just segment by segment
  • Confirm who's responsible for each transition, not just each segment
  • Build in buffer time around anything involving VIPs or guest speakers

Materials & Supplies

  • List everything that needs to be printed, built, or bought, with an owner and a deadline for each
  • Have a backup for anything that can't be replaced last-minute — signage, certificates, giveaways

People

  • Assign one point person per area — registration, program, technical, logistics
  • Make sure every volunteer knows who to contact if something goes wrong, not just what their own task is

Contingency

  • Identify what happens if the weather changes, a speaker cancels, or turnout is different than expected
  • Know who has the authority to make a call on the day, so no one is guessing

A checklist won't prevent every surprise, but it removes the surprises that were preventable — which, in most events, turns out to be most of them.

Building an Interview Rubric That Actually Works

Team & Recruitment

Interviewing based on gut feeling works fine for one or two candidates. It falls apart the moment you're evaluating dozens of applicants across multiple interviewers, because “I liked them” isn't a standard — it's a mood, and it's different for everyone on the panel.

A rubric fixes this by turning vague impressions into comparable scores. The first step is defining the competencies that actually matter for the role before writing a single interview question — things like communication, initiative, reliability, or relevant experience — rather than starting with questions and hoping they reveal something useful.

Not every competency deserves equal weight. A recruitment role might weight communication heavily and technical skill lightly; a finance role might be the reverse. Deciding the weighting in advance keeps the panel from unconsciously favoring whichever trait each individual interviewer happens to value most.

Behavioral questions tend to produce more comparable answers than hypothetical ones. “Tell me about a time you had to manage a conflict on a team” reveals more than “how would you handle a conflict,” because it forces the candidate to describe something that actually happened rather than what they think sounds good.

Scoring should happen independently before the panel discusses the candidate. If interviewers compare notes first, the most confident voice in the room quietly becomes the rubric. Independent scores, then a short calibration discussion afterward, keep the process fair and catch cases where one interviewer scored unusually high or low.

A good rubric doesn't remove judgment from interviewing — it makes that judgment consistent, defensible, and fair to every applicant going through the same process.

Onboarding New Members Without Overwhelming Them

Team & Recruitment

The instinct after recruitment is to hand new members everything at once — the org chart, the full history, every policy, every channel — in a single orientation session. Almost none of it sticks, because people can only absorb so much before it turns into noise.

Information needs sequencing. New members need to know, on day one, exactly what they need in order to function this week: who their point of contact is, where to find the next meeting, and what their first task actually is. Everything else — long-term structure, past projects, org history — can wait until it's relevant.

Pairing each new member with a buddy or mentor, someone slightly more experienced who isn't their direct supervisor, gives them a low-pressure person to ask “obvious” questions to. People are far more likely to ask a peer than to interrupt a meeting to admit they're lost.

An early, small, genuinely useful task matters more than a long welcome packet. Reading materials build familiarity; doing something builds confidence and a sense of belonging. Give new members something real to contribute within their first week or two, even if it's small.

A living reference document — updated as processes change — beats a one-time orientation every time. Orientations happen once and get forgotten. A shared, searchable doc lets new members find what they need on their own schedule, months after the day they joined.

Good onboarding isn't about front-loading information. It's about giving people just enough to start, and making it easy to find more when they actually need it.

What to Do When Your Program Runs Long

Problem‑Solving Under Pressure

Almost every event runs long somewhere. The events that recover gracefully aren't the ones that avoided delays entirely — they're the ones that planned, in advance, for what to do when a delay happened.

The first step happens before the event: identify which segments are essential and which are flexible. Not every part of a program carries equal weight. Knowing in advance what can be shortened or cut — and what absolutely cannot — means that decision doesn't have to be made from scratch, under pressure, in the moment.

One person should have clear authority to make real-time calls on the schedule. If three people are quietly deciding whether to cut a segment, the delay gets worse while they sort out who's in charge. A single point of decision, agreed on beforehand, keeps the response fast.

When changes happen, communicate them clearly to whoever needs to know — the emcee, the technical team, the next presenter — rather than letting people find out mid-segment. A quiet scramble backstage is far less noticeable to an audience than a visibly confused handoff on stage.

Transition moments are usually the easiest place to absorb lost time, not the content itself. Trimming a walk-on, a long introduction, or an extended round of applause recovers minutes without cutting anything the audience actually came for.

Protect the ending more than the start. A program that starts a few minutes late and ends on time is barely noticed. A program that starts on time and drags past its scheduled end is what people remember.

Staying Steady When Everything Changes Last Minute

Problem‑Solving Under Pressure

There's a particular kind of stress that comes from a plan falling apart the night before it's supposed to happen — a venue issue, a key person dropping out, a detail that was assumed handled and wasn't. How a leader responds in that moment sets the tone for everyone around them.

The first useful move is separating the problem from the panic. The situation is a logistics issue that needs a decision. The feeling of everything falling apart is a natural reaction, but it isn't the same thing as the actual scope of what's wrong — and treating them as the same thing usually makes the response bigger than the problem requires.

Triage matters more than speed. Not everything needs to be solved in the next ten minutes. Sorting problems into what needs a decision right now versus what can wait until tomorrow prevents a single evening from turning into a crisis on five fronts at once.

It helps to loop in only the people who are actually needed to solve the specific problem, rather than alerting the whole team immediately. Widespread panic doesn't add capacity — it usually just multiplies the number of people who are also now stressed and waiting for direction.

A leader's tone gets copied by the team almost automatically. Calm, direct communication — even when the situation genuinely isn't fine yet — tends to produce a calm, focused response from everyone else. Visible panic tends to produce more of the same.

Save the full debrief for after the event, not during the crisis. In the moment, the job is to solve the problem. What went wrong, and how to prevent it next time, is a conversation worth having later, when there's room to actually learn from it instead of just reacting.
