Managing a Team vs. Leading One

Leadership Fundamentals

Every student organization runs on some mix of management and leadership, but the two aren't the same skill. Management keeps a team on schedule. Leadership gives a team a reason to stay on schedule when it stops being easy.

Management is task-oriented. It's assigning roles, tracking deadlines, following up on deliverables, and making sure logistics don't fall through the cracks. It's necessary — a group with no management structure will miss deadlines no matter how inspired everyone feels. But management alone treats people like items on a checklist, and checklists don't ask why they're doing something.

Leadership is direction-oriented. It's explaining why a task matters, not just that it's due Friday. It's noticing when someone is quietly burning out before they say anything. It's the difference between “submit the design by Friday” and “here's why this design has to land — it's the first thing new members will see, and it sets the tone for the whole event.” Same deadline, very different reason to care.

Most student leaders default to management under pressure because it's measurable — you can point to a spreadsheet and see what's late. Leadership is harder to track, which is exactly why it gets skipped when things get busy. But teams that only experience management tend to do the minimum required and disengage the moment no one's watching.

The fix isn't choosing one over the other — it's noticing which one you default to and deliberately practicing the other. If you're naturally good at rallying people but bad at tracking deadlines, build a simple tracking system. If you're naturally organized but rarely check in on how people are doing, block ten minutes before every meeting just to ask.

5 Signs Your Event Timeline Is Too Tight

Event Planning Essentials

A packed program looks impressive on paper. The problem is paper doesn't account for late attendees, a microphone that needs troubleshooting, or a segment that runs four minutes over because the crowd was into it. Here are five signs your timeline needs more breathing room before it's tested live.

  1. Every task has exactly one person who can do it. If your emcee is also your tech operator and your only person who knows the Wi-Fi password, one delay anywhere becomes a delay everywhere. Tight timelines have no redundancy built in.
  2. Setup and rehearsal share the same block. If your schedule assumes the venue will be fully set up and the run-through finished in the same 30 minutes, one of those two things is going to get cut short — usually the rehearsal, which is the one that actually catches mistakes.
  3. There's no transition time between segments. Real programs don't move from one segment straight into the next with zero gap. People need to walk on and off stage, hand off microphones, and reset. A timeline with zero transition buffer is a timeline that's already behind before the event starts.
  4. Vendor and supplier arrival times are assumed, not confirmed. "They should be here by 8" is not the same as a confirmed delivery window. If your timeline depends on an external party showing up exactly on schedule, and that hasn't been confirmed in writing, the plan is built around a hope.
  5. There's no answer for what happens if something runs ten minutes over. If the honest answer to "what do we cut if we're running late" is "we'll figure it out," that's the clearest sign the timeline is too tight — because the plan already assumes it might not hold.

None of this means over-engineering every minute. It means building in small amounts of slack on purpose, in the places most likely to slip, so a single delay doesn't cascade into the whole program falling apart.

Onboarding New Members Without Overwhelming Them

Team & Recruitment

The instinct after recruitment is to hand new members everything at once — the org chart, the full history, every policy, every channel — in a single orientation session. Almost none of it sticks, because people can only absorb so much before it turns into noise.

Information needs sequencing. New members need to know, on day one, exactly what they need in order to function this week: who their point of contact is, where to find the next meeting, and what their first task actually is. Everything else — long-term structure, past projects, org history — can wait until it's relevant.

Pairing each new member with a buddy or mentor, someone slightly more experienced who isn't their direct supervisor, gives them a low-pressure person to ask “obvious” questions to. People are far more likely to ask a peer than to interrupt a meeting to admit they're lost.

An early, small, genuinely useful task matters more than a long welcome packet. Reading materials build familiarity; doing something builds confidence and a sense of belonging. Give new members something real to contribute within their first week or two, even if it's small.

A living reference document — updated as processes change — beats a one-time orientation every time. Orientations happen once and get forgotten. A shared, searchable doc lets new members find what they need on their own schedule, months after the day they joined.

Good onboarding isn't about front-loading information. It's about giving people just enough to start, and making it easy to find more when they actually need it.
